Muhurtha

A Hindu calendar that actually explains itself. Muhurtha gives you today's Panchang — tithi, nakshatra, yoga, karana and vaar — with Rahu Kaal and Abhijit as plain times you can act on, 96 festivals across ten regional traditions, eclipse visibility from where you stand, Pitru Paksha shraddha days, and a muhurat finder for a wedding, a housewarming or a new car. Every term has a tap-to-read explanation in ordinary English, so you never have to already know what Ekadashi means to use it. Everything is computed on your phone from the positions of the Sun and Moon. No account, no ads, no network, and nothing to buy.

Panchang means "five limbs". Most apps show you all five and explain none.

Muhurtha shows the same five, and tells you what each one is for.

TithiThe lunar day. Changes when the Moon gains another 12° on the Sun — so it rarely starts at midnight.
NakshatraWhere the Moon is sitting tonight, out of 27 star-groups. This is the "my nakshatra" people mean.
YogaSun and Moon added together. Twenty-seven of them; a handful are worth avoiding.
KaranaHalf a tithi. Two per day, and one of them — Vishti — is the one elders tell you to sit out.
VaarThe weekday, but measured sunrise to sunrise. Before dawn still counts as yesterday.
Today

One screen that answers "is now a good time?"

The day opens with what is actually happening: a fast, a festival, someone's tithi birthday. Then Plan your day — one sentence naming the next good window and how long until it opens, and the hours to leave alone.

Tap anything and it explains itself. What Pradosh is for. Why Rahu Kaal is an eighth of the daylight and not a fixed 90 minutes. What the 13th moon day has to do with Shiva.

Windows of the day

And when a good hour and a bad one collide, it says so.

Abhijit sits at midday. On a Wednesday, Rahu Kaal sits there too. Most apps print both in green and red and leave you to notice. Muhurtha strikes the good one out, says which window overrode it, and stops offering it as the day's best.

Any auspicious Choghadiya that runs into Rahu Kaal, Yamaganda, Gulika or Dur Muhurat is dropped the same way — so the windows you are shown are the ones that survive.

The year

96 festivals, dated from the Moon — not from a list someone typed in.

Every festival in Muhurtha is a rule: a tithi, in a month, judged at the moment tradition judges it. Diwali at twilight, Janmashtami at midnight, Ganesh Chaturthi at midday. Change city and the dates move, because for some years and some places they genuinely do.

Pick the traditions you actually follow and the rest disappear — including Tulu Nadu's own calendar, which almost nothing else carries.

Muhurat

A year of dates for the things people plan around a Panchang.

Wedding, griha pravesh, a vehicle, property, a new venture, a naming. Muhurtha walks the year, keeps the days whose tithi, nakshatra and weekday all clear the traditional shortlist, and shows you the hours within each one — with Rahu Kaal, Yamaganda, Gulika and Dur Muhurat already cut out.

Weddings and housewarmings respect Kharmas and Chaturmas. Buying a car does not, because tradition never asked it to.

It is a starting list, not a verdict. A family priest also weighs the couple's own charts and the lagna at the intended hour — neither of which an almanac alone can supply, and the app says so on the screen.

You & family

The birthday the Panchang keeps.

Enter a birth — date, time and place — and Muhurtha works out the nakshatra and its pada, the moon and sun rashi, the tithi, the month, and the syllable a name traditionally starts with.

Then it tells you when that tithi next comes round. A janma tithi birthday, or an annual shraddha, lands on a different Gregorian date almost every year, which is exactly why families lose track of them. Muhurtha puts a card on Today when one arrives.

Ask

Ask it in your own words. It answers offline.

"When is Diwali 2026?" "What is Ekadashi?" "Rahu Kaal today." "Festivals in October." Muhurtha reads the question, works the answer out from its own Panchang, and offers to open that day or read the story behind it.

There is no server and no model call. The assistant is rules over the same engine the rest of the app uses, which is why it works on a plane and why nothing you type leaves your phone.

Solar and lunar eclipses with local visibility. If it happens below your horizon the app says so plainly, and notes that tradition holds sutak does not apply where an eclipse cannot be seen.

Ekadashi with its parana window, Pradosh, Sankashti with the moonrise it actually depends on, Purnima and Amavasya — each with what it is for and how it is kept.

Where the numbers come from

Drik Ganita — computed positions, not tabulated approximations.

Muhurtha carries its own ephemeris, written in Swift: a truncated VSOP87D for the Sun and ELP-2000/82 for the Moon, with nutation, ΔT and sidereal time after Meeus. Sidereal longitudes use the Lahiri (Chitrapaksha) ayanamsa, the same one the Indian civil calendar uses. Tithi, nakshatra, yoga and karana boundaries are found by root-finding on the real angles rather than by dividing a day into equal parts.

The engine is checked against Drik Panchang for New Delhi — all five limbs, both karanas, sunrise and sunset, the months and the samvats — and that comparison is a permanent test, so it cannot drift. Eclipse times and magnitudes are checked against the NASA canon. Nothing is fetched. There is no calendar to download and no server to be down; the app works the same in a basement or on a flight.

Elevation is off by default, so sunrise matches what published panchangs print. Amanta is the default month system and Purnimanta is one tap away. The whole method is written out inside the app, under Settings.

What it is, and what it isn't

Worth being straight about before you install anything.

It is

  • A Panchang for someone who was never taught one — every term explained, in the place you meet it.
  • Entirely offline. No account, no analytics, no network calls, nothing leaves the phone.
  • Free, with no upsell. There is no Pro tier and there won't be one.
  • Honest about regional difference: pick your tradition, and see when two of them disagree.
  • Written for the diaspora too — the dates shift correctly for San Francisco or Sydney, because the Moon does not care where you moved.

It isn't

  • Astrology. There is no kundli, no chart reading and no prediction — only what the almanac itself records.
  • A replacement for your family priest, least of all for a wedding muhurat.
  • Finished in every language. English is complete; Hindi, Marathi, Gujarati, Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam are partway through and fall back to English until a native speaker has signed them off.
  • Authoritative on every regional observance. Where a date came from a published source rather than a computed rule, the app says which.

Nobody ever explained it to me either.

Every family has someone who knows when Ekadashi falls and why you don't start something during Rahu Kaal. Ours did. But that knowledge travels badly — it lives with one generation, in one town, and the rest of us end up checking a website that prints the numbers without a word about what they mean.

Muhurtha is the version I wanted: the same numbers a good panchanga gives you, computed properly rather than copied, with a sentence of plain English next to every one of them. If you grew up with it, the dates should match what your family keeps. If you didn't, you should be able to pick it up without asking anyone.

Free, offline, no account. Like everything else here.
