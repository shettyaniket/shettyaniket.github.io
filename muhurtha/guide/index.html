User Guide

Muhurtha, explained

Muhurtha is a Hindu calendar and Panchang that assumes you were never taught one. It gives you the same numbers a good printed panchanga gives — computed from the actual positions of the Sun and Moon, not copied from a list — and puts a sentence of plain English next to every one of them.

This guide walks through the whole app. You do not need to read it in order; if you only want to know what Rahu Kaal is and when to avoid it, jump to Plan your day.

Your first minute

There is no sign-up and no onboarding questionnaire. The app opens on today.

  1. 1
    Allow location, or pick a city

    Every number in a Panchang depends on your sunrise, so the app needs to know where you are. Allow location once, or tap the city pill under the date and choose one by hand — search works offline against a bundled list, and reaches Apple's geocoder only if you look for somewhere it doesn't carry.

  2. 2
    Read the first two cards

    Anything notable about today comes first — a fast, a festival, an eclipse. Then Plan your day tells you, in one sentence, when the next good window opens and which hours to leave alone.

  3. 3
    Tap anything you don't recognise

    Every term in the app opens a short explainer: what it is, what tradition says it's for, and what people do and avoid. That is the whole point of the app.

  4. 4
    Pick your traditions

    Tap the gear on Today → Which festivals to show. Ninety-six festivals is a lot if half of them aren't yours. Turn on the regions you actually keep.

Today

Plan your day

This is the card most people open the app for. It answers one question — is now a good time to start something? — and it answers it relative to the current moment.

The first line names the window you are in, or the next one and how long until it opens. The second line is Rahu Kaal: on now, coming up in so long, or already over. Below them sit the day's windows as chips you can read at a glance.

The green windows

WindowWhat it is
AbhijitRoughly 48 minutes centred on local midday — the "victorious" muhurat, traditionally good for almost anything.
Amrit KaalDerived from the nakshatra, about 96 minutes. Considered especially favourable.
ChoghadiyaThe day split into eight parts, each named. Amrit, Shubh, Labh and Char are the usable ones.
Brahma MuhuratThe 48 minutes ending shortly before sunrise. For study, prayer and anything that wants a quiet mind.

The red ones

WindowWhat it is
Rahu KaalOne eighth of the daylight, in a slot that depends on the weekday. The best known of them.
YamagandaAnother eighth, another weekday slot. Same idea.
Gulika KaalAnd a third. Often treated as the mildest of the three.
Dur MuhuratOne or two fifteenths of the day, by weekday. Short and specific.
VarjyamA nakshatra-derived stretch to avoid, about 96 minutes.
🕐
These are not fixed clock times

Rahu Kaal is an eighth of the time between your sunrise and your sunset. It is not "always 4:30 to 6:00 on a Saturday". Move city, or move six months through the year, and it moves with you — which is exactly why an app that computes it beats one that tabulates it.

Today

When Rahu Kaal wins

Good and bad windows are worked out separately, so they sometimes land on top of each other. The most common case is Wednesday: Abhijit sits at midday, and Wednesday's Rahu Kaal is the fifth eighth of the day, which also sits at midday.

Muhurtha treats the red window as overriding. When that happens you will see:

⚠️
Rahu Kaal sits on top of Abhijit today

So Abhijit does not count. Use another window.

The Abhijit chip stays on the card but goes grey, its hours are struck through, and it carries a line saying which window overrode it. Rahu Kaal takes the highlight instead. The headline will never offer you an eclipsed window as the day's best.

Auspicious Choghadiya and Amrit Kaal are handled the same way, except that they are dropped from the card entirely rather than shown struck through — one Labh window is much like another, so a dead one is only clutter, whereas Abhijit's silent absence at midday would look like a bug.

The five limbs

Panchang literally means five limbs. Scroll down on Today and you'll find all five with their start and end times. Every one of them is tappable.

LimbWhat it measuresWhy you'd care
TithiThe Moon gaining 12° on the Sun. Thirty per lunar month, fifteen per paksha.Every festival and fast is dated by one.
NakshatraThe Moon's position among 27 star-groups, each 13°20′ wide.Your "birth star". Also decides Amrit Kaal and Varjyam.
YogaSun and Moon longitudes added, divided into 27.A few — Vyatipata, Vaidhriti — are traditionally avoided.
KaranaHalf a tithi. Two each day.Vishti (Bhadra) is the one to sit out.
VaarThe weekday, sunrise to sunrise.Decides where Rahu Kaal, Yamaganda and Gulika fall.
🌅
The day begins at sunrise

This trips everyone up. If something happens at 3 AM on Thursday morning, the Panchang still calls it Wednesday. It matters for birth details, for fasts, and for which tithi "today" is.

Favourable yogas

These are combinations of weekday and nakshatra that tradition treats as lucky in their own right. They are the usual reason to check a Panchang on an ordinary Tuesday rather than only around festivals.

Sarvartha Siddhi Amrit Siddhi Ravi Yoga Guru Pushya Ravi Pushya

Sarvartha Siddhi — "accomplishing every purpose" — is the broadest. Guru Pushya is Pushya nakshatra landing on a Thursday, and is the day people buy gold. Amrit Siddhi is rarer and is double-edged in some traditions, which the app notes rather than hiding.

The card shows which are running, when they start and end, and a line on what each one is.

Changing the place or the date

A different day

The arrows either side of the title step a day at a time. Tap the year beside the date to jump to any date at all. "Today" returns you to the present.

A different place

Tap the city pill. You can use your current location, pick from cities you've saved, or search. The bundled list covers India, the diaspora and the wider Hindu world and works with no network; anything else is geocoded by Apple on your device if you're online.

✈️
The dates really do move

If you're in New Jersey and your family is in Mangaluru, some festivals genuinely fall on different days for the two of you — because the tithi has to be running at a particular moment of the local day. Switch the city and watch it happen. That's not a bug in either calendar.

Pan-Hindu North India Maharashtra Gujarat Bengal & East Tamil Nadu Kerala Karnataka Tulu Nadu Andhra & Telangana

Tap the count beside a tradition to see exactly which festivals it would add before you commit — no guessing from the name. There's also a switch for minor observances, if you want the well-known days only.

Bisu Parba, Aati Amavasya, Keddasa and Pattanaje are in here, dated against the Tulu solar calendar. Very little else carries them.

Year

When two days disagree

Some years a festival genuinely falls on two different days depending on where you are and which rule your community follows. Diwali is the classic case: it wants Amavasya at twilight, and some years the tithi straddles the boundary.

Rather than silently picking one, Muhurtha flags the day and explains the alternative — which other date has a claim, and why. If your family keeps the other one, you'll at least know why the app said what it said.

⚖️
A real example

Diwali 2024 was contested in Delhi and not in San Francisco. Same festival, same rule, different sunsets.

What it checks

A day has to clear three lists at once — the tithi, the nakshatra and the weekday — each of them specific to the purpose. Weddings and housewarmings also respect the seasons tradition sets aside: Kharmas, when the Sun is in Sagittarius or Pisces, and Chaturmas, Vishnu's four months of sleep. Buying a car is not bound by either, so the app doesn't pretend it is.

The hours it gives you

Tap a day to open it. The app works out the stretches when a shortlisted tithi and a shortlisted nakshatra are both actually running, then cuts out Rahu Kaal, Yamaganda, Gulika and Dur Muhurat, rejoins anything a tithi change had split, and discards fragments under 45 minutes. What's left is what you're shown, with the nakshatra running through each stretch.

🙏
A starting list, not a verdict

A family priest also weighs the couple's own charts and the lagna at the intended hour. Neither of those is something an almanac alone can supply. Use this to narrow a year down to a handful of candidate dates, then take those to whoever your family asks.

If you don't know the time

Untick "I know the time" and the app assumes sunrise, which is where the Panchang day starts anyway. The tithi and rashi will usually still be right; the nakshatra can be one step out, because the Moon moves through one roughly every 24 hours. The app says so on the person's page rather than quietly presenting a guess as fact.

🔒
It stays on your phone

Names, dates and places are stored on the device and never sent anywhere. There is no server to send them to.

Personal

Tithi birthdays

Your janma tithi is the birthday the Panchang keeps: the same tithi, in the same lunar month, coming round each year. Because the lunar year is about eleven days shorter than the solar one, it lands on a different Gregorian date almost every year — which is exactly why families forget it.

Muhurtha works out the next one and puts a card on Today when it arrives. It's computed where you are now, not where you were born, because a birthday is kept where you live.

📅
Adhika months are skipped

A leap month doesn't carry an anniversary, so the app looks past it to the real one.

Personal

Shraddha

Add someone as a Remembrance rather than a birthday and the app tracks their varshik shraddha — the annual tithi on which they passed — the same way.

Separately, the Year tab lists all sixteen days of Pitru Paksha with the tithi each one belongs to, and Today shows the kutup and aparahna windows when one falls.

Set up

Amanta or Purnimanta

North and south India disagree about when a lunar month ends. Amanta months end at the new moon and are used across the south and west. Purnimanta months end at the full moon and are used across the north.

The festivals land on the same days either way — only the month name for the dark fortnight differs. Muhurtha defaults to Amanta and shows both on a person's birth reading. Switch in the Year tab's options, or see which you're on in Settings.

Under the hood

How it's worked out

Muhurtha uses Drik Ganita — computed positions — rather than the older tabulated approximations. It carries its own ephemeris written in Swift:

PartMethod
SunTruncated VSOP87D, accurate to about one arcsecond.
MoonELP-2000/82, accurate to about ten arcseconds.
Nutation, ΔT, sidereal timeAfter Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms.
AyanamsaLahiri (Chitrapaksha) — the one the Indian civil calendar uses.
Limb boundariesRoot-finding on the real angles, not equal division of a day.
EclipsesMeeus chapter 54, with local visibility from your horizon.

The engine is checked against Drik Panchang for New Delhi — all five limbs, both karanas, sunrise and sunset, the months and the samvats — and that comparison is a permanent automated test, so it can't quietly drift. Eclipse times and magnitudes are checked against the NASA canon.

Elevation is off by default, because published panchangs compute sunrise at sea level and turning it on would put the app a couple of minutes out from what your family is reading. You can switch it on if you want the physically accurate time for a hill station.

📴
Nothing is downloaded

There's no calendar to fetch and no server to be down. The app computes 1900 to 2100 from first principles, on your phone, in milliseconds.

FAQ

Why don't the dates match the calendar on my wall?

Usually the month system or the tradition. Check whether your calendar uses Amanta or Purnimanta, and whether the festival you're looking at belongs to a region you've turned off. If it's a genuinely contested date, the app will say so on the day.

Why is Rahu Kaal a different time than I remember?

Because it's computed from your sunrise and sunset, not looked up. It moves with the city and with the season. The remembered "always 4:30 on a Saturday" is a rule of thumb for one latitude at one time of year.

Does it need internet?

No. Never, for anything in the Panchang. The only optional network call in the app is Apple's geocoder, and only if you search for a city that isn't in the bundled list.

Will it drain my battery?

No. A day's Panchang takes a few milliseconds to compute and nothing runs in the background except the notifications you asked for.

Is there a kundli or horoscope?

No, and there won't be. Muhurtha reports what the almanac records. It makes no predictions and reads no charts.

What years does it cover?

1900 to 2100. Everything in that span is computed, not tabulated.

Can I use it outside India?

Yes — that's a large part of why it exists. Pick your city and the whole calendar re-derives for your sunrise. The bundled city list covers the diaspora specifically.

Is my data backed up?

Saved places and family details live on the device only. There is no cloud sync, so reinstalling the app starts fresh.

Known limits

Worth being honest about.

  • Languages. English is complete. Hindi, Marathi, Gujarati, Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam are partway through and fall back to English for anything not yet translated, until a native speaker has reviewed them.
  • Devotional texts. The aartis and chalisas need a proofread against an authoritative printed source before they can be called final. The Hanuman Chalisa is currently partial.
  • Naming syllables. The nakshatra-pada table is the commonly published one, but families use it to name children, so it deserves checking against a printed source rather than being taken on trust.
  • A few regional dates. Keddasa and Pattanaje are placed from published dates rather than from a computed rule, and the Tulu month-start convention follows the Tamil one. A local panchanga should have the last word.
  • Solar eclipse visibility. The app can tell you the Sun is above your horizon at greatest eclipse, but not whether you're inside the shadow path — so it says exactly that, rather than claiming a visibility it can't establish.
  • No lagna. Muhurat suggestions are day-level and window-level. The rising sign at a particular hour is a priest's job.

Privacy

No accounts, no analytics, no ads, no tracking SDKs, no server. Locations, family details and preferences are stored on your device and never transmitted. Notifications are scheduled locally. Calendar access is write-only. There are no purchases.
